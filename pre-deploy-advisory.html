01 — The Problem

Procurement Built On Smoke And Mirrors

Most equipment failures in the field aren't mechanical. They're decisions made too early, with too little ground truth, by people whose only job was to close the sale.

01
Equipment is purchased based on manufacturer specification sheets — not on how that equipment performs in your heat, dust, altitude, terrain, or EW environment.
02
Decision-makers with limited UAS or C4ISR technical depth have no way to separate genuine performance data from misleading or selectively-framed statistics.
03
By the time a capability gap is discovered, the budget is spent, the equipment is on-site, and the mission — not the vendor — absorbs the cost.
04
Procurement teams become the scapegoat for a failure that was avoidable from day one — with the right independent assessment, before signing.
02 — The Assessment

Four-Pillar Site Evaluation

A specialist team — project manager and technical lead — spends one week on-site per location, assessing the conditions equipment will actually have to survive.

Infrastructure
Power, connectivity, hangar and storage, maintenance facilities, transport, and logistics access.
Mission Goals
What the operation actually needs equipment to achieve — and where current plans diverge from that.
Environmental Factors
Terrain, climate, dust and thermal cycling, altitude, and electronic warfare / GPS-denied conditions.
Supporting Elements
Staff skill levels, training requirements, and the operational support structure around the equipment.

The aircraft had been delivered; the capability had not.

While operating a mission-proven UAV platform, our team noticed twelve aircraft sitting idle in the same hangar — representing a $20M contract.

A brief technical discussion with the on-site team surfaced concerns about environmental suitability and operational requirements. The fleet had never successfully entered service.

The aircraft had been delivered. The capability had not.

This is the cost of getting requirements wrong. An independent technical assessment is measured in thousands. Discovering the same oversights after delivery is measured in millions.

$5M
Acting as Field Service Representative, our team was asked to weigh in on a $5M intelligence-gathering UAV purchase nearing final approval. A specifications review surfaced maintenance, sustainment, and long-term support requirements the platform couldn't meet in the client's operating environment. We advised against the acquisition — helping the client avoid committing significant resources to a platform carrying substantial long-term sustainment risk.

While supporting a client under a Field Service Representative (FSR) contract, our team was asked for a quick opinion on a proposed $5M intelligence-gathering UAV purchase nearing the final stage of procurement.

A review of the aircraft's specifications surfaced concerns beyond performance figures — maintenance requirements, sustainment demands, and long-term support considerations that raised real questions about whether the platform could be effectively operated within the client's environment and available resources.

We advised against proceeding with the acquisition in its current form.

By evaluating the system through a lifecycle and operational lens — not just a capability lens — the client avoided committing significant resources to a platform carrying substantial long-term sustainment risk.
